Book review #1: Heaven by Mieko Kawakami

The review may contain spoilers ahead.

<!--more-->

I came across Heaven by Mieko Kawakami almost by accident while
browsing the Audible catalogue one day. I hadn’t read any of her work
before, but a quick search and the summary caught my attention, partly
because bullying is a subject I have encountered often in Korean and
Japanese popular media. I was curious to see how the treatment of it
would be in literature specifically. I’m glad to have found the book
because I ended up connecting with it more deeply than I had expected.
I think some books reveal their central themes within the span of a
couple of chapters, but Heaven wasn't one of them. Instead, it unfolded
through constant tensions between competing ideas, often leaving me
questioning the conclusions and my own interpretations. I'll try to
summarise the book below, hoping to document my thoughts and
introspections.

Instead of starting this review with the first chapter, I want to begin
with a song by the same name, “Heaven” by Talking Heads, which came to
mind while listening to Chapter 5. One particular line

> Heaven is a place where nothing ever happens

stayed with me because I find it very relatable. The song presents
heaven not as some grand, transcendent Eden, but as a painfully
ordinary and repetitive place. It is a place without growth, surprise,
or consequence, but as a result, it is also a place without suffering.
This image of permanence can be either deeply comforting or unsettling,
depending on how we choose to look at it, but it certainly stands in
contrast to our world, where causes have effects, actions have
consequences, and suffering is an inescapable part of existence.
This led me to think that perhaps the intangible heaven we aspire for
does not exist at all and perhaps we are simultaneously living in our
heaven and hell, and this existence is the only reality we will ever
have.

Kawakami herself writes something similar,

> Listen, if there’s a hell, we’re in it. And if there’s a heaven,
> we’re already there. This is it.

However grim it may sound, it is also extremely grounding. There is no
promise of another world in which everything will eventually make
sense. There is only this present one. The characters in Heaven have no
choice but to live within that reality, as they attempt to understand
it. I think this makes the book such a compelling read. In this world,
there is little that changes, and things are caught in an endless
cycle. School begins, pain and humiliation repeat, school ends, and the
next day begins again. The same people occupy the same hierarchy and
the same power dynamic. There are events, but no real progress, and no
guarantee that the suffering will lead to anything better. The
characters are left with the world as it is and the difficult task of
making sense of it. This is where Heaven becomes an exploration of
what happens to our ideas of morality when suffering becomes ordinary,
whether suffering has any inherent meaning, and whether enduring
suffering is virtuous.

The narrator of the book is a fourteen-year old boy whom his
classmates call “Eyes” and relentlessly bully. The dehumanising aspect
of it is visible in just the nickname, which reduces a complete person
to a single physical feature. The bullying is led by Ninomiya, who,
while socially and academically successful, subjects the narrator to
the daily routine of humiliation and cruelty. This has become an
ordinary part of the narrator's life and an inseparable component of
the school experience for him. The cycle of violence has become so
routine for him that the anticipation of it is itself a part of the
violence. Then there is his classmate Kojima, who in a similar vein is
mocked and treated as "dirty" by her classmates. She deliberately lets
her appearance separate her from her peers rather than trying to blend
in.

The novel begins when she and the narrator start passing notes to each
other in secret. This leads to a miraculous friendship, perhaps not
unexpected, as neither of them has to explain to the other the basic
experience of being treated like an “outsider”. This shared yet
harrowing experience gives them something to mutually bond over. This
friendship is also perhaps the first appearance of something resembling
the heaven we aspire to in the novel. It's simply a place where we are
treated as complete persons, we are understood, and we can bond over
shared experiences. Together, they build a small private world within
the larger hostile one. They exchange notes, arrange secret meetings,
and eventually visit a museum together to see a painting that Kojima
considers significant. Within this world, the narrator is no longer
simply “Eyes”, and Kojima is no longer simply the "dirty" girl everyone
avoids. They can exist outside the identities imposed upon them.

This friendship also gives them a place from which they can begin to
understand their suffering differently, as Kojima gradually builds a
philosophy around it. She comes to believe that weakness has meaning
and that their position as outsiders is a kind of moral vocation. If
the world humiliates them, then there is value in enduring it without
becoming like those who hurt them. Her suffering becomes part of her
identity, and the marks of her mistreatment become almost sacred. There
is something understandable and even comforting in this idea because it
gives her control over the meaning of something she cannot control in
reality. But it also creates a danger. If suffering becomes the
foundation of her identity, then escaping or resisting it could mean
losing the very thing that gives it meaning.

This is where Nietzsche comes to mind. In his discussion of slave
morality, Nietzsche describes how people who lack the power to act in
the world can reinterpret their powerlessness as a form of moral
superiority. The strong become cruel, while the weak become virtuous,
patient, and morally superior

> The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of
> resentment becoming creative and giving birth to values—a resentment
> experienced by creatures who, deprived as they are of the proper
> outlet of action, are forced to find their compensation in an
> imaginary revenge.

He connects this transformation to ressentiment, where an inability to
act outwardly against one's circumstances turns inward and becomes a
system of values through which those circumstances are reinterpreted.
However, Kawakami does not allow us to accept this philosophy
comfortably. Kojima's interpretation of suffering gives her dignity and
something to hold onto, but it can also become a trap. If suffering
gives us meaning, then resisting it begins to feel like giving up that
meaning. The novel therefore asks whether the need to make suffering
meaningful can itself become a way of accepting injustice, rather than
a reason to fight the conditions that cause it. The narrator is
therefore caught between two very different ways of understanding his
own suffering. He is drawn to Kojima's interpretation, but at the same
time he cannot completely accept the core idea that suffering is
inherently valuable.

Then there is Momose, one of Ninomiya's followers, who actively
participates in the bullying of the narrator. His presence makes the
novel's philosophical conflict more apparent because he approaches the
question of suffering without any need to find meaning in it. He is
intelligent, composed, and detached, and unlike Kojima, he does not try
to transform suffering into something morally significant. His position
is essentially that people do things because they can. There may be no
deeper moral reason behind suffering, no heaven in which good is
rewarded, no hell in which evil is punished, and no inherent meaning
behind the power one person has over another. This brings Nietzsche to
mind again, although Momose's philosophy is deliberately a much more
nihilistic version of Nietzsche's ideas. Nietzsche's concept of the will
to power is not simply a justification for the strong to dominate the
weak. His broader idea was concerned with self-overcoming, the creation
of values, and what happens when we can no longer rely on an external
moral order to tell us what is good.

> What is good? - All that heightens the feelings of power, the will to
> power, power itself in man. What is bad? - All that proceeds from
> weakness. What is happiness? - The feeling that power increases - that
> a resistance is overcome.

This may sound close to Momose's worldview, but Nietzsche's idea of
power is not the ability to dominate others but the capacity for
self-overcoming and creating one's own values, something that Momose
reduces to the much simpler idea that the absence of an external moral
order gives one permission to do whatever one can. This leaves the
narrator stuck between two incomplete answers. Kojima tells him that
suffering can be given meaning, while Momose tells him that it has no
meaning at all, and the narrator cannot fully accept either position.
The ending does not resolve this conflict with some revelation or
triumph over the bullies. Instead, the narrator is forced to confront
the possibility that suffering may not contain any inherent meaning,
while still recognising that this does not make his experience
meaningless. The important thing is what he chooses to do with all
this experience and how he responds.

The conflict becomes much more concrete after the narrator is severely
beaten by the bullies, to the point that he has to go to the hospital
for treatment and begins suffering from insomnia. When he visits the
doctor, he learns that his “unnatural eye”, which until then has been
the defining feature of his identity, can actually be “treated” very
easily. This should have been an uncomplicated piece of good news. Yet
when he tells Kojima that his eye can be “fixed”, she strongly objects.
She cannot accept the idea of him removing something that has come to
represent the beginning of their relationship and the meaning they have
attached to their shared suffering. The narrator’s decision to have it
“fixed” therefore feels to her almost like a rejection of the meaning
she has constructed around their suffering, and ultimately contributes
to the breakdown of their now romantic relationship. The tragedy is that
the narrator is finally presented with a genuine opportunity to change
his circumstance, but doing so comes at the cost of losing the one
person who had treated him as a complete and equal human being.

Shortly after this, the bullies force Kojima to contact the narrator and
arrange a meeting under the pretence of reconciling their differences.
Starved of connection, the narrator agrees, only for the meeting to
become the final escalation of their dehumanisation. The bullies,
led by Ninomiya, force Kojima and the narrator to undress, stand
completely naked, and perform sexual acts as a spectacle for their
mindless amusement. The scene is extremely difficult to read because it
strips away the last remnants of the philosophical meaning they had
attached to their suffering, the private "heaven" they had created
together, and ultimately even their humanity.

The scene ultimately culminates in the adults discovering the narrator
and Kojima naked and intervening, with Kojima being taken away from
him. The narrator then returns home to his mother, who does not
question him about what happened or demand an explanation, despite the
obvious social implications of a boy and a girl standing naked. Instead,
she simply takes care of him and helps him finally have his eye
“fixed”. Her response is strikingly ordinary. Like Momose, she does not
attempt to attach any deeper meaning to his “condition”; as to her, it is
simply something that can be medically treated. This practical response
inadvertently cuts through the philosophical significance the narrator
and Kojima had attached to it. The procedure therefore marks the end of
their relationship, their shared world, and the narrator’s attempts
to identify with his suffering itself.

The ending of Heaven is left deliberately unresolved. It does not offer
suffering as a lesson or meaninglessness as an answer. It leaves the
narrator, and the reader, with the harder possibility that suffering may
simply happen, without a reason, and that meaning is something we have
to construct and choose how to respond to it. This is also where Heaven
differs significantly from the familiar structure of Western young-adult
fiction. In many stories about bullying there, adolescence becomes a
battlefield to be won. The protagonist is bullied, discovers some hidden
strength, finds allies, confronts the bully, and eventually emerges with
a clearer sense of self. The cruelty is answered because the story
itself promises some form of resolution. Kawakami does not seem to be
interested in providing that satisfaction. After Heaven, such
resolutions also seem to feel philosophically cheap.

Ninomiya is not a convenient monster, but someone who is socially
successful, intelligent, and well regarded by his peers. Momose is
similarly secure and detached. The people doing the hurting are not
outsiders themselves, but people who have succeeded within the same
hierarchy that permits the bullying to happen. There is no obvious
antagonist to defeat and no simple moment when justice is restored. The
adults are no more reassuring; institutions simply fail, quietly, and
all life continues around that failure. This also makes Heaven feel
less like a conventional coming-of-age story and more like an
examination of what it means to live within a world that does not
guarantee resolution. By refusing the familiar young-adult promise,
Kawakami leaves the narrator with something much harder and deeper.

Another aspect of the novel that stood out to me is how young all of
its characters actually are. The narrator, Kojima, Ninomiya, and Momose
are only middle schoolers, yet they frequently speak, reason, and
philosophise with a degree of self-awareness that feels extremely adult.
This is something I have noticed in Japanese media more broadly, where
children and adolescents are often given an unusually sophisticated
interiority and made to confront questions that would ordinarily be
associated with adulthood. Hōrō Musuko, for example, similarly treats
its young characters not simply as children passing through adolescence,
but as people capable of grappling seriously with self-identity, gender
identity, gender dysphoria and alienation. This makes the characters
feel strangely caught in a world between childhood and adulthood where
they remain subject to the powerlessness and cruelty of childhood, while
being forced to develop a philosophical sense suited for adults. This
tension makes their suffering even more unsettling, as they are expected
to understand a world they are still too young to have any real power
within.

I have not experienced bullying on anything like the scale the
narrator does, but I recognised the attempt to attach meaning to
suffering and the difficulty of overcoming it. In my case, that
suffering has at times taken the form of extreme solitude, sometimes
of my own making. When I failed to climb out of it, and eventually
failed even to try, I began constructing various internal philosophies
to justify and accept my state. There is a strange comfort in doing
this, almost a feeling of being special. If suffering differentiates
a person from everyone else, it becomes easier to believe that there
is something valuable in the very thing that is hurting them. The
problem is that climbing out then becomes harder, because admitting
that loneliness is simply an unfortunate circumstance also means
admitting that it could be changed, and that possibility comes with
an obligation. It asks something of me, often the uncomfortable task
of changing aspects of my own character or behaviour, which is much
harder than explaining the loneliness away. This is what makes Kojima's
philosophy particularly recognisable to me. Her suffering becomes part
of her identity, and the meaning she gives it eventually becomes so
intertwined with who she is that imagining herself outside of it
becomes almost as difficult as imagining herself without the suffering
itself.

Recognising that trap, however, is easier than escaping it.
Understanding why someone has built a philosophy to make suffering
bearable does not necessarily make it easier to abandon that
philosophy. Insight can become another form of hiding, as a person
becomes so articulate about their condition, why they stopped trying,
or why their circumstances make sense given everything that came
before, that the explanation itself begins to feel like progress.

The way forward, I think, has to begin with understanding one's own
condition, recognising the character traits and internal philosophies
that have allowed a person to accept a situation that needs to change,
and then either working to change them or recognising the need to reach
out for help.
