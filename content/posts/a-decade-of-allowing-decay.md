---
title: "A decade of allowing decay"
date: 2026-09-13T11:47:19+05:30
---

I want to preface this by saying this is going to be a
retrospective political essay with my personal feelings and opinions on
various matters. It might not be a good read for everyone unless they
also come from the same state and system as me.

<!--more-->

I was trying to find a good introduction for this post for quite some
time, as I was planning to write this for long. I was playing a couple
of options in mind, trying to settle on it, but since this is going to be
a political post, I decided today to settle on the moment in my life
when I officially entered the electorate.

I don't remember much of the details since it was more than a decade
ago, and it was quite a mundane event for me at that time. It was so
mundane that I didn't even care since a high schooler in his final
years isn't expected to participate in political discourse. I had
assumed that it was just another official document I would need later in
life. I was naive and grew up to be apathetic partly because my
parents didn't really want me to participate. They never said that out
loud, but you could obviously sense it. This shielding is perhaps not
surprising considering how much people value education and material
success it brings in life and how dirty politics is here, but perhaps
it is surprising given how political the society and surroundings
actually are.

I cannot hope to encompass the entire fervour, but I shall say there's
some election happening every year or so in which we are supposed to
participate; there's political discourse happening inside tea shops
every five feet apart and inside buses between random men who never met
before. There's some protest or rally going on every day, and there are
political student unions in every university participating in all of
those above and then some more.

The next part may even be more surprising when I say that for a society
with so much political participation, we haven't progressed much,
whether in terms of economic growth or social growth. The subject
experts can better analyse the reasons. I never studied them
academically. So I will resort to making a couple of perhaps broad
generalisations based on my own experience and opinions.

I think people are aware that we are the birthplace of several major
religions and that we have nearly two thousand distinct languages in
use. Language, religion, customs, and even everyday social norms really
do change dramatically within a few hundred kilometres. I'm personally
trilingual, but I would be completely lost culturally and in terms of
communication if I travelled to certain parts of the country.

We were founded on the principles of secularism, mutual respect, and
"unity in diversity". I was taught in school that, despite the immense
diversity, it is this shared sense of unity that brings us together and
gives us a common sense of belonging. Learning about this felt
genuinely "cool" and special when I was younger. I now realise that we
were shielded from the reality that there is also a huge amount of
deeply rooted, unspoken inter-community and inter-faith hostility, and
that this supposed unity is remarkably fragile whenever something
falls outside one's own "community". It's difficult to even define what
exactly constitutes a community here. Sometimes it means people of the
same religion; sometimes people of the same religion and of the same
social status on top; and sometimes it can even be something as
small as belonging to the same family.

I don't know exactly how we arrived at this point. Some of it is
undoubtedly historical, some of it has been passed down through
generations, and some of it may simply be the result of people
inheriting thoughts and prejudices without ever questioning where they
came from. Often, there isn't even a conscious decision to hold these
beliefs. People just grow up with them, absorb them, and continue
passing them on.

Anyway, coming back to the present, I notice this every day. Sometimes
online, sometimes in real life, and I've come to believe that this lack
of unity and the latent hatred have been a major hindrance to social and
political progress. I don't think people collectively vote with the
long-term improvement of their surroundings in mind. More often, it
seems to come down to short-term benefits such as government
subsidies, social benefits, or whatever promises a politician makes to
pander to a particular electoral community and secure their votes. This
gives them enough of an edge where they don't really have to care about
people from other communities.

This creates a particularly destructive cycle. Disunity, political
apathy, and inter-community hatred create an environment in which
corruption can thrive. Governments change, but people's lives as a whole
remain unchanged. Elections can be won by pandering to specific
communities, securing particular vote banks, exploiting or
cultivating existing inter-community hatred, and offering enough
freebies or targeted benefits to keep those constituencies satisfied.
The result is a political system that rewards division and short-term
thinking rather than long-term reform. In that sense, it can feel like
a travesty of democracy. A democracy can only produce meaningful
change if its voters collectively demand it. If the electorate does not
choose to prioritise the right things, those things are not going to
happen, regardless of who is in power.

I shall admit that I played my own part in this. I knew better, but I
didn't vote. Elections went by while I sat at home, hating everything
and trying to be a closeted ideologist. Part of that was undoubtedly
apathy and a sense that there was no real collective will for change.
However, the bigger reason was that I never found a single political
party here whose ideology I could genuinely stand behind. I have very
strong ideological beliefs of my own. So I kept asking myself how I could
I vote for someone who doesn't represent what I believe. I could never
quite bring myself to choose the lesser evil, because to me, the lesser
evil was still evil. So I opted out entirely.

So I watched quietly as the right wing took power year after year by
appealing to the broad religious identity and exploiting and/or
actively cultivating the existing inter-community hatred. It gave them
the largest and most powerful vote bank ever in terms of raw numbers
and also because religion here is not merely an abstract belief. It is
deeply intertwined with people's everyday lives, social identities,
family structures, customs, and their sense of status and belonging. It
is as tangible as it can get.

Once in power, they steadily weakened state institutions after
institution, replacing independent or democratically accountable
positions with party loyalists, while presenting the transfer of
social welfare and economic opportunity toward capital as necessary
for industrial growth. At the same time, their public-facing political
wing cultivated a softer, more culturally palatable form of religious
nationalism, while their extremist wing worked to cultivate the hardline
religious beliefs and embed them within institutions such as the
education system. Their long-term vision is like a puritanical
theological society in which individual freedoms and particularly
women's rights or even basic human rights become subordinate to
religious and social conservatism.

And while all of this was happening, the public sphere itself changed
rapidly. Religious festivals, holidays, saints, sages, and processions
increasingly became more politically and culturally prominent than
say students protesting for their rights or ordinary people protesting
over social and economic issues. Public harassment and lynching members
of the other community became increasingly common.

Much of this was and still is ultimately hidden behind a singular
political strongman. He has been the face of every election, the
saviour who stood above the ugliness of everything surrounding him. His
supporters revere him and can separate him and the party from the
institutions being degraded, from the extremists operating in plain
sight, and from the consequences of the policies being implemented by
his government.

People love their protagonists and antagonists in movies; they love
their pop stars, their larger-than-life characters. So naturally
demagoguery thrives. His face is everywhere. On newspapers, on public
transport, on advertisements, on subways, trains and buses.

Even though they were elected to power at the centre and in almost
every state, a few states remained outliers. Mine was one of them,
despite being the birthplace of the founder of the right-wing
ideological movement. Of course, no one actually cares about his
ideals anymore; his name is largely invoked as a symbol to legitimise
the extremist political project.

We had a socialist government, followed by a communist, and eventually
a liberal one, but never a right-wing one. It took a long time for the
political tide to reach here. But when almost the entire country is
already within their grasp, the scent was eventually bound to reach here
too. And so it did.

I was appalled. They initially tried to present themselves as
centrists, but it did not take long for the extremist fangs to show.
One of our universities consistently ranks among the finest in the
country, but it has also historically been a stronghold of socialist
and communist politics. They have never been particularly comfortable
with that even when they were the opposition. Once in power, there
seems to be an almost instinctive desire to eliminate dissenting
voices by whatever means available. So party cadres were mobilised to
attack the students living in the university accommodation in the middle
of the night, while religious hardliners were brought in to picket the
gates, block access, and chant religious texts in an attempt to create
chaos and intimidation. New regulations were introduced by the state
that made it possible to remove particular student political leaders
from the university, and more recently, job transfers were mandatory,
creating a mechanism through which dissenting professors and university
officials could potentially be displaced.

The broader objective seems clear to me - remove dissenting voices, turn
the university into a new party stronghold, and gradually undermine an
institution built around independent thought and critical inquiry. Once
that happens, education becomes another sector that can be opened up to
political patronage and private capital, while the very culture of
critical thinking that makes a university valuable is slowly dismantled.

They have followed the exact same playbook elsewhere in the country to
much success. I have little reason to not believe that this time will
be different.

All of this leaves me sad and hopeless that this is the reality I have
to wake up to every day. I sometimes find myself wondering how we
arrived at this point, and today I am writing about the conclusions I
arrived at through introspection. I don't think history will forgive us
for what we allowed to happen. It certainly won't forgive me, because
I failed to exercise the rights I had. While they were becoming
entrenched in their puritanical and extremist ideology, I was sitting
at the opposite extreme. I hold myself responsible for the political
and social decay that I caused, and the fact that generations after me
will have to live with the consequences of that apathy.

I can only hope that, by the time they are finished, there is still
enough of a democratic apparatus left for the electoral outcome to be
undone. I learnt from my mistake, and I hope to play my part now, even
if that means swallowing the concept of "lesser evil".

This post isn't a call to action. I'm too much of a coward to go out
into the streets and fight for what I believe in. This is simply a
personal retrospective, serving no other purpose than to document my
feelings. I may face repercussions for
publishing this later in life. It has happened numerous times before
here, but it won't change my views or my principles.

I want to end with a play on a quote I've always liked.

Tyranny does not require constant effort when we are collectively
apathetic.
